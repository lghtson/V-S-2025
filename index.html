Are Film Rankings Actually Objective?

Usually, film rankings feel quite objective.

Lists like IMDB's Top 250 - as seen below - make it seem like there's a hierarchy which could be described as the "best" films ever made. The Shawshank Redemption (1994) has topped this list since 2008.

However, with so many platforms for users to rate these films, it can be rather inconclusive.

Different styles of art appeal to different audiences, and audiences utilising differing platforms question the placement of these "best" movies. In addition, different rating systems produce different ideas of "quality". Harakiri (1962) would be considered as the greatest movie of all time according to Letterboxd, with The Shawshank Redemption being moved down to 7th. There are 7 movies with a perfect 100 critic score via MetaCritic (Citizen Kane, Vertigo, Casablanca, Rear Window, Lawrence of Arabia, Tokyo Story, The Godfather).

So... What if we combine these systems?

And rather than having a pre-defined "best" list, how about choosing a different starting film that changes the shape of the ranking? In this way we can find more films that are similar to our favourites.

Notice how selecting a crime drama like The Godfather produces a very different list compared to an animated film like Princess Mononoke.

What does the data reveal?

However, it's crucial to note that this project isn't attemptting to produce the “true” best films. Instead, it treats ranking as a constructed system. By combining platform scores and genre similarity, the project shows how different data sources create different versions of cultural value.
